\begin{abstract}
Production pipelines that deploy Large Language Models (LLMs) as structured data extractors often assume that identical inputs yield identical outputs; however, this assumption does not hold in practice. The same model, given the same prompt and input, can produce JSON records that vary structurally or semantically across runs, a phenomenon termed \emph{output volatility}. A common mitigation strategy is the \emph{validate-and-retry} loop, in which schema-violating outputs are rejected and validator feedback is iteratively provided to the model until compliance is achieved. This work evaluates the effectiveness of this approach. A live Travel Risk Assessment pipeline is constructed to synthesize U.S.\ State Department advisories and real-time news into structured JSON risk reports for 30 countries. An unconstrained baseline is compared against a schema-validated retry condition across two model scales: a 120B-parameter LLM (\texttt{gpt-oss-\allowbreak 120b}) and an 8B-parameter Small Language Model (SLM, \texttt{llama-3.1-\allowbreak 8b}), both evaluated at temperature $T{=}1.0$. The hypothesis that schema constraints reduce volatility by at least 50\% is rejected. Constraints yield an 11.9\% stability improvement for the SLM (statistically significant) and 4.8\% for the LLM (not significant). Additionally, the 8B SLM demonstrates 26--29\% greater stability than the 120B LLM across all conditions, suggesting a capacity-consistency tradeoff. Retry mechanisms incur a 30--40\% increase in token usage, further diminishing their effectiveness. These results indicate that structural validation alone is insufficient for achieving production-grade consistency, and that semantic canonicalization remains a critical unresolved challenge.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

% ─── Paragraph 1: The Problem ───
Consider a production dashboard that ingests LLM-generated country risk assessments every morning. On Monday, the pipeline reports that Mexico's active risk categories are \{\texttt{crime}, \texttt{kidnapping}\}. On Tuesday---same model, same prompt, same underlying advisory and news inputs---it reports \{\texttt{crime}, \texttt{civil\_unrest}, \texttt{other}\}. No ground truth has changed. The model has simply sampled a different output from its distribution. This is \emph{output volatility}: the failure of an LLM to return equivalent structured records across independent runs on identical inputs. The problem is fatal for automated information extraction (IE) pipelines. Analysts, databases, and downstream dashboards cannot distinguish a resampling artifact from a genuine change in the world, which means every alert is suspect and every trend line is potentially noise. As LLMs become the default engine for structured data extraction in domains from financial compliance to medical coding to geopolitical risk, volatility stops being a curiosity and becomes a production liability.

% ─── Paragraph 2: The Gap ───
The dominant engineering response is the \emph{schema constraint architecture}: intercept the model's output with a JSON schema validator, and if the response violates the schema, feed the validator's exact error message back to the model as corrective feedback in an auto-retry loop \cite{willard2023efficient, beurer2023prompting}. This ``validate-and-retry'' pattern has become a default production recipe and is frequently marketed as a path to near-deterministic structured output. Prior work on structured output largely endorses this direction. Shrimal et al.\ \cite{shrimal2025parse} report a 92\% reduction in extraction errors using reflection-based retry, and benchmarks such as JSONSchemaBench \cite{geng2025jsonschemabench} measure how reliably models comply with schemas on a single call. What this literature does not measure is the metric that actually matters for production pipelines: whether the same input produces the same output across independent runs. Structural compliance on one run is not the same as consistency across many, and the gap between the two has not been quantified on a realistic, multi-source IE task.

% ─── Paragraph 3: The Contribution ───
This paper quantifies that gap. The contribution is a live, multi-source \textbf{Travel Risk Assessment Pipeline} that synthesizes U.S.\ State Department advisories and real-time news into structured JSON risk reports for 30 countries, paired with a controlled stability evaluation across two model scales (120B-parameter LLM, 8B-parameter SLM) and two conditions (unconstrained baseline vs.\ schema-validated retry). All inputs are cached at session level so that observed variance reflects model stochasticity rather than input drift. Stability is measured as the agreement between structured records produced on independent runs of the same input. The central research question is whether schema validation with retry reduces output volatility by at least 50\%---a principled ``majority contribution'' benchmark under which constraints would be the dominant stabilization mechanism, responsible for more improvement than all remaining sources of variance combined.

% ─── Paragraph 4: Results Preview ───
The 50\% threshold is robustly rejected. Schema constraints reduce volatility by only 11.9\% for the SLM (statistically significant) and 4.8\% for the LLM (not statistically significant, within sampling noise). More strikingly, the smaller 8B SLM is 26--29\% \emph{more stable} than the 120B LLM across every tested condition---a capacity-consistency tradeoff that inverts the intuition that larger models produce more reliable structured outputs. Retries also consume 30--40\% additional tokens, further eroding the value proposition of structural guardrails. The remaining volatility is dominated by a \emph{canonicalization gap}: semantically equivalent strings (e.g., \texttt{"Arauca"} vs.\ \texttt{"Arauca Department, Colombia"}) that validators cannot collapse but that downstream consumers treat as distinct. Production IE pipelines that require robust consistency cannot rely on schema enforcement alone; semantic grounding must be treated as a first-class component of the reliability architecture.

% ─── Contributions list (kept separate, follows the 4-paragraph arc) ───
The core contributions of this paper are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A reproducible multi-source geopolitical risk IE pipeline with session-level input caching and SQLite-backed provenance logging, enabling controlled stability evaluation.
  \item A quantitative comparison of constrained vs.\ unconstrained generation across two model scales, using Row Jaccard Similarity with bootstrap confidence intervals.
  \item Empirical evidence of a \emph{capacity-consistency tradeoff}: smaller models are more stable than larger ones at high temperature in structured IE settings.
  \item Identification of the \emph{canonicalization gap}---the class of semantic volatility that purely structural constraints cannot address---with concrete engineering directions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This work sits at the intersection of three research threads: non-determinism and volatility in LLM outputs, structural enforcement of generated content, and LLM-based information extraction. Each thread has produced useful findings, but none has directly measured whether the dominant production technique---schema validation with error-feedback retry---meaningfully reduces cross-run output volatility in a realistic IE task. This paper fills that measurement gap.

\subsection{Non-Determinism and Output Volatility}

LLM unreliability has been studied most extensively through the lens of hallucination---the generation of factually incorrect or fabricated content \cite{ji2023survey}. A related but distinct failure mode has received less attention: \emph{structural volatility}, or inconsistency in the form and categorical choices of outputs across repeated inferences on identical inputs.

Two recent findings motivate this paper directly. Song et al.\ \cite{song2024good} demonstrate that evaluation benchmarks reporting a single output per example systematically understate true performance variance, and argue that non-determinism must be treated as a first-class concern. Atil et al.\ \cite{atil2024nondeterminism} extend the concern to supposedly deterministic settings: $T{=}0$ generation with fixed seeds still fails to guarantee identical outputs at scale, because floating-point non-associativity in parallel GPU computation introduces irreducible noise. Together, these results establish that volatility is real and cannot be eliminated by configuration alone---which is precisely why schema-level stabilization mechanisms have become the default production response. Camuffo et al.\ \cite{camuffo2025variance} add a related diagnosis: prompt phrasing and model selection can shift annotation outcomes by 12--85 percentage points, placing a large residual burden on post-generation guardrails.

These papers establish the problem. None evaluates whether schema-level guardrails actually solve it. This paper provides that evaluation.

\subsection{Structural Enforcement: Proactive and Retroactive}

Two broad strategies exist for enforcing structured output from LLMs. \emph{Proactive} approaches intervene during generation, masking token logits at each step to guarantee grammar or schema conformance \cite{willard2023efficient}. Query languages such as LMQL formalize this as a programming paradigm \cite{beurer2023prompting}, and Geng et al.\ \cite{geng2025jsonschemabench} benchmark compliance rates across constrained decoding engines. These approaches guarantee syntactic validity but not semantic correctness---and Tam et al.\ \cite{tam2024let} show that format restrictions can actively \emph{degrade} task performance by forcing the model away from its highest-probability natural language completions. Deutsch et al.\ \cite{deutsch2025constrained} extend this finding across 11 models, observing that instruction-tuned models suffer more than base models under constraints.

\emph{Retroactive} approaches---schema validation with error-feedback retry---are more common in production but less studied empirically. The closest prior work is Shrimal et al.\ \cite{shrimal2025parse}, whose PARSE system reports a 92\% reduction in extraction errors within the first retry. The metric in that work is structural compliance on a single run. This paper evaluates the less-studied but arguably more important question: whether validation-and-retry improves output \emph{consistency across independent runs} on identical inputs. The distinction matters because single-run compliance can be high while cross-run consistency remains low---exactly the situation this paper's results document.

\subsection{Information Extraction with LLMs}

LLMs have become the dominant paradigm for universal information extraction (UIE), replacing earlier rule-based and supervised approaches \cite{li2024simple}. Two findings from this literature shape the present work. Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2024we} identify 134 use cases for output constraints in LLM applications and categorize them into low-level (format, length) and high-level (semantic, stylistic) requirements, concluding that addressing only structural constraints leaves a large residual of semantic violations unhandled. This observation directly motivates the canonicalization gap analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:error}. Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2022self} propose Self-Consistency, aggregating multiple sampled outputs via majority vote as an alternative to structural enforcement; the approach improves reliability but multiplies inference cost linearly with sample count, making it impractical for high-throughput production settings. The pipeline evaluated here targets the opposite regime: single-inference-per-input, where structural guardrails are the only available stabilization mechanism.

\subsection{Geopolitical Risk and NLP Pipelines}

NLP-based geopolitical risk assessment has received growing attention, typically producing time-series indices from news articles via sentiment analysis and named entity recognition \cite{caldara2022measuring}. These systems target aggregate signals rather than structured, per-country risk schemas. The pipeline presented here is distinct in three respects: it combines live multi-source ingestion (state advisories plus news), it targets a strict categorical schema, and it evaluates per-run consistency as a first-class metric. Together these choices make it a harder and more practically relevant testbed for LLM output stability than prior geopolitical NLP work.

\section{System Architecture and Data Pipeline}
\label{sec:architecture}

The pipeline is designed around a single experimental constraint: all observed output variation must reflect model stochasticity, not input drift. This section describes how data sources, caching, schema enforcement, and logging are composed to meet that constraint.

\subsection{Data Sources and Ingestion}

The pipeline operates over two complementary data sources:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{U.S.\ State Department Travel Advisories.} Official country-level risk assessments written as verbose prose covering security conditions, criminal activity, natural hazards, and regional restrictions. Advisories are fetched and cached via a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server to avoid redundant API calls and guarantee reproducibility across runs.

  \item \textbf{Real-Time News Articles.} Recent geopolitical and security-relevant articles retrieved via a news API and filtered per country. News provides time-sensitive context not captured in the relatively static advisory prose. News data is also cached at the session level.
\end{enumerate}

Caching is load-bearing for the experiment. Without it, identical prompts issued on different days would see different news contexts, and any cross-run variation could be attributed to feed drift rather than model behavior. Session-level caching freezes both sources at the start of each experimental run and holds them constant across all 600 inference calls, guaranteeing that the only variable between runs is the model's sampling process.

\subsection{Target JSON Schema}

Both sources are synthesized into a single structured JSON record conforming to a strict schema. The fields relevant to the stability evaluation are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{country} (string): ISO country identifier.
  \item \texttt{risk\_level} (integer, 1--4): State Department advisory level, from Level~1 (Exercise Normal Precautions) to Level~4 (Do Not Travel).
  \item \texttt{event\_types} (array of enum): Categorical risk flags drawn from a fixed vocabulary of \{\texttt{crime}, \texttt{terrorism}, \texttt{civil\_unrest}, \texttt{kidnapping}, \texttt{natural\_disaster}, \texttt{health}, \texttt{conflict}, \texttt{other}\}.
  \item \texttt{regional\_warnings} (array of strings): Free-form named regions or areas with elevated risk.
  \item \texttt{summary} (string): Brief prose synthesis.
\end{itemize}

The schema deliberately combines two types of fields with different volatility profiles. The \texttt{event\_types} field is categorically constrained: a finite enum, validator-enforceable, and the primary locus of \emph{structural} volatility. The \texttt{regional\_warnings} field accepts free-form strings and is the primary locus of \emph{semantic} volatility---the class of inconsistency that schema validators cannot detect. Evaluating both fields within a single record allows the experiment to measure where structural enforcement succeeds and where it reaches its limits.

This logging architecture enables post-hoc metric computation and error analysis across any subset of runs without requiring re-inference.

\subsection{Prompt Design}

The system prompt is frozen and identical across all runs for a given model. It instructs the model to: (1) synthesize the advisory and news content for the target country; (2) return a JSON object conforming to the schema exactly; and (3) use only the specified vocabulary for \texttt{event\_types}. In the constrained condition, the retry prompt appends the literal validator error---for example, \texttt{"invalid value 'cartel\_violence' in event\_types; valid options: [crime, terrorism, civil\_unrest, kidnapping, natural\_disaster, health, conflict, other]"}---together with the original prompt and a regeneration instruction. This design ensures that the retry mechanism communicates both the constraint and the specific violation, giving the model the maximum possible information to self-correct.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Models and Configuration}

Two models representing substantially different scales are evaluated:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{gpt-oss-\allowbreak 120b}} (LLM): OpenAI's open-source 120B-parameter instruction-tuned model, accessed via the University of Florida HiPerGator AI API endpoint.\footnote{\url{https://api.ai.it.ufl.edu/v1}} This model represents the high end of commercially available API models.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{llama-3.1-\allowbreak 8b}} (SLM): Meta's Llama 3.1 8B-parameter open-weight instruction-tuned model, also accessed via the HiPerGator API. This represents the class of efficient, production-deployable models.
\end{itemize}

Both models are configured at $T{=}1.0$ to maximize output entropy. The choice is deliberate. The goal is to stress-test stabilization mechanisms at their logical limits, not to demonstrate the trivial result that low-temperature generation is more consistent \cite{atil2024nondeterminism}. All other generation parameters (\texttt{top\_p}, \texttt{max\_tokens}) are fixed identically across conditions to isolate the effect of schema enforcement.

\subsection{Experimental Conditions}

Two conditions are applied to both models:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Unconstrained (Baseline).} The model generates a response directly from the prompt. The output is parsed as JSON for storage and metric computation, but no validation or retry is applied. Structurally malformed outputs are logged as-is.
  \item \textbf{Constrained.} The output is passed through a strict JSON Schema validator. If validation fails---missing keys, wrong types, or hallucinated enum values---the validator serializes the specific error and triggers a retry. The error message is appended to the conversation context as corrective feedback. The loop continues until the output passes validation or a maximum of 3 retry attempts is exhausted.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluation Scope}

The experiment covers \textbf{30 target countries} spanning all major geographic regions and advisory risk tiers (Level 1: Exercise Normal Precautions through Level 4: Do Not Travel). The country set was fixed prior to any data collection to avoid selection bias. Five independent runs are generated per country per condition per model, yielding $5 \times 2 \times 2 \times 30 = 600$ baseline inference calls plus additional retry calls in the constrained condition.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric: Row Jaccard Similarity}

The primary metric is the \textbf{Row Jaccard Similarity}, which measures the overlap between two structured records on a scale from 0 (no shared content) to 1 (identical content). Formally, for a pair of output records $(A, B)$:

\[
  J(A, B) = \frac{|S_A \cap S_B|}{|S_A \cup S_B|}
\]

where $S_A$ and $S_B$ are \emph{typed token sets} constructed from each record. For a record $R$:
\[
S_R = \left\{
  \begin{aligned}
    &\texttt{event\_type:}v \mid v \in R.\texttt{event\_types} \\
    &\cup\ \texttt{regional\_warning:}v \mid v \in R.\texttt{regional\_warnings}
  \end{aligned}
\right\}
\]

The type prefixes prevent spurious collisions between fields (e.g., a string \texttt{"health"} appearing in both \texttt{event\_types} and \texttt{\texttt{regional\_\allowbreak warnings}} is counted as two distinct tokens). The formulation has two properties that matter for this experiment. First, it is invariant to list ordering within JSON arrays, so purely cosmetic reorderings do not artificially depress stability. Second, it captures both \emph{addition volatility} (the model introduces a new token in a later run) and \emph{deletion volatility} (the model drops a token it previously included), which together characterize the inconsistency that matters for downstream consumers.

\paragraph{Worked example.} Consider two runs for Mexico producing the following records:

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Run A:}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize, frame=none, backgroundcolor=\color{gray!5}]
event_types = [crime, kidnapping]
regional_warnings = ["Tamaulipas"]
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent\textbf{Run B:}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize, frame=none, backgroundcolor=\color{gray!5}]
event_types = [crime, civil_unrest]
regional_warnings = ["Tamaulipas", "Michoacan"]
\end{lstlisting}

\smallskip
\noindent The typed token sets are:
\begin{align*}
S_A = \{\,&\texttt{event\_type:crime},\ \texttt{event\_type:kidnapping},\\
         &\texttt{regional\_warning:Tamaulipas}\,\} \\[4pt]
S_B = \{\,&\texttt{event\_type:crime},\ \texttt{event\_type:civil\_unrest},\\
         &\texttt{regional\_warning:Tamaulipas},\\
         &\texttt{regional\_warning:Michoacan}\,\}
\end{align*}

The intersection has 2 elements, the union has 5, so $J(A, B) = 2/5 = 0.40$. A score of 1.0 would mean the two runs produced identical records; 0.0 would mean they shared no content.
By combining the categorically constrained \texttt{event\_types} and the free-form \texttt{regional\_warnings} in a single metric, the Row Jaccard score captures both structural and semantic volatility. This makes it a deliberately conservative measure of stability---sensitive to the canonicalization failures analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:error}.

For a country with $n$ runs, the Row Jaccard Similarity is computed for all $\binom{n}{2}$ pairwise run comparisons and averaged to produce a stability score for that country-condition cell. The overall stability score for a model-condition pair is the mean across all 30 countries.

\paragraph{Excluded scalar fields.} The \texttt{risk\_level} field is excluded from the metric because it is passed through from the State Department advisory and the prompt instructs the model to echo it unchanged. Its variance does not reflect the kind of categorical structural volatility this experiment is designed to measure.

\paragraph{Excluded free-text fields.} The \texttt{advisory\_summary}, \texttt{entry\_exit\_\allowbreak requirements}, \texttt{news\_headlines}, and other prose fields are excluded because exact string equality is an inappropriate standard for free text. These fields are analyzed via secondary metrics (cosine similarity of sentence embeddings) rather than Jaccard.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

To assess whether observed stability differences are statistically meaningful rather than artifacts of sampling variance, \textbf{95\% Bootstrap Confidence Intervals} (BCIs) are constructed for all reported means using $B = 1{,}000$ bootstrap resamples of the country-level stability scores \cite{efron1994introduction}. A 95\% BCI is a range within which the true population mean is estimated to lie with 95\% probability, given the observed sample. Non-overlapping BCIs between two conditions indicate that the observed difference is unlikely to be due to chance (statistically significant at approximately the 5\% level).

\paragraph{Volatility reduction metric} The volatility reduction is defined as the proportion of residual instability that schema constraints eliminate:
\[
  \text{Reduction} = \frac{\bar{J}_{\text{constrained}} - \bar{J}_{\text{unconstrained}}}{1 - \bar{J}_{\text{unconstrained}}}
\]
where $\bar{J}$ denotes the mean Row Jaccard Similarity across all countries. A value of 50\% indicates that constraints close half the gap between observed baseline stability and perfect reproducibility ($J{=}1.0$). This residual-improvement formulation is more informative than a simple relative change: it measures progress toward the practical goal of full consistency, rather than relative change against an arbitrary baseline.

The SLM's stability advantage is not a marginal effect: it represents a 26--29\% absolute improvement over the LLM in mean Jaccard Similarity. This finding contradicts the intuition that larger models, being generally more capable, would produce more consistent structured outputs.

We hypothesize that this inversion arises from a \textbf{capacity--consistency tradeoff}: the 120B model's vast expressive capacity enables it to generate semantically richer and more contextually nuanced outputs, but this same capacity produces a flatter, wider output distribution that resists stabilization. The 8B model operates with a narrower effective hypothesis space, making its categorical choices more \emph{predictable} even at high temperatures.

This is consistent with recent work by Yuan et al.\ \cite{yuan2025numerical}, who demonstrate that large models distributed across multiple GPUs introduce more floating-point nondeterminism than smaller models that fit on a single device, and with observations by Song et al.\ \cite{song2024good} that larger models do not consistently outperform smaller ones on stability-sensitive evaluation tasks.

\subsection{Constraint Benefit Scales with Model Size (Inversely)}

A related finding is the differential benefit from constraints across model scales. Table~\ref{tab:benefit} quantifies the \emph{relative benefit multiplier}---the ratio of percentage improvement in the SLM vs.\ LLM from applying constraints.

\subsection{Type 1: Structural Violations}

Structural violations are malformed outputs that a JSON schema validator can detect and trigger a retry for. Observed examples include: missing required keys (the \texttt{event\_types} array omitted entirely), hallucinated enum values (\texttt{"cartel\_violence"} instead of one of the eight valid event types), and type mismatches (\texttt{risk\_level} returned as a string instead of an integer).

These violations are significantly more common in llama-3.1-\allowbreak 8b than in gpt-oss-\allowbreak 120b. In the SLM's unconstrained condition, approximately 18\% of generations contained at least one structural violation. In the LLM's unconstrained condition, the rate was approximately 6\%. This 3$\times$ gap explains directly why the SLM benefits from retry loops and the LLM does not: the LLM simply produces fewer Type 1 failures for the validator to catch. The residual volatility in the LLM's outputs is therefore concentrated in Types 2 and 3, which schema enforcement cannot address.

\subsection{Type 2: Categorical Interpretation Variance}

Even when outputs are structurally valid, the same underlying advisory text can elicit different categorical assignments across runs. A report of ``organized crime groups operating in border regions'' may be tagged \texttt{crime} in one run and \texttt{terrorism} in another, depending on subtle differences in how the model parses the prose at $T{=}1.0$. Both assignments are defensible interpretations; neither is a schema violation; the validator has no basis to prefer one over the other.

This form of volatility is partially addressable by a more sophisticated retry loop---one that encodes semantic rules (e.g., ``cartel-related activity must map to \texttt{crime}, not \texttt{terrorism}''). The current implementation does not implement such rules, so Type 2 errors pass through the validator untouched. Quantifying their exact rate would require ground-truth category labels for each input, which are not available for this corpus.

\subsection{Type 3: Canonicalization Failures}

The most pernicious form of volatility affects the \texttt{regional\_warnings} free-form string list. Schema constraints enforce that this field is an array of strings, but they cannot enforce that semantically equivalent strings are represented identically. Across runs, the same geographic entity appears under multiple surface forms:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{"Arauca"} vs.\ \texttt{"Arauca department"} vs.\ \texttt{"Arauca Department, Colombia"}
  \item \texttt{"FATA region"} vs.\ \texttt{"Federally Administered Tribal Areas"} vs.\ \texttt{"tribal border areas"}
  \item \texttt{"Northern Border"} vs.\ \texttt{"northern border states"} vs.\ \texttt{"Mexico-US border region"}
\end{itemize}

A human reader recognizes all three variants in each group as references to the same underlying place. A set-based Jaccard comparison does not: it treats \texttt{"Arauca"} and \texttt{"Arauca Department, Colombia"} as entirely distinct tokens, counting each occurrence against stability. This canonicalization failure is fundamentally a semantic problem, not a structural one. Resolving it requires a downstream normalization layer---either a named entity linker (NEL) that maps surface forms to canonical geographic IDs, or few-shot canonicalization examples embedded in the prompt itself. Neither is provided by a schema validator.

Type 3 failures dominate the residual volatility in both models' outputs and are the primary reason the 50\% hypothesis was rejected. No amount of structural validation reduces them.

\subsection{Bimodal Stability in the LLM}

A striking characteristic of the gpt-oss-\allowbreak 120b results is the width of its 95\% bootstrap confidence interval: [0.30, 0.62] unconstrained and [0.33, 0.64] constrained. This 0.32-unit spread is itself a diagnostic finding. The LLM is not uniformly unstable. It exhibits \emph{bimodal} stability behavior: near-identical outputs on some countries, radically divergent outputs on others.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/gpt_per_country.png}
\caption{Per-country mean Row Jaccard Similarity for gpt-oss-\allowbreak 120b (unconstrained vs.\ constrained). The spread from near-zero to near-perfect stability reveals input-dependent bimodality that structural constraints do not smooth.}
\label{fig:gpt_percountry}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:gpt_percountry} visualizes this dispersion. Countries with well-documented, unambiguous risk profiles cluster at the high-stability end---both low-risk countries with short advisories (NZL, NOR) and high-risk countries with clear, narrow advisories (PRK, SYR). Countries with complex, multi-faceted risk landscapes (CHN, MEX, IND) fall at the low-stability end, where the LLM diverges substantially between runs.

The pattern suggests that the LLM's output distribution is highly sensitive to the semantic complexity of the input. Simple inputs produce tight, reproducible output distributions; complex inputs produce flat, high-entropy distributions that no amount of structural validation can narrow. This is precisely the capacity-consistency tradeoff identified in Section~\ref{sec:results}, visible at the per-country level. The SLM does not exhibit the same bimodality because its narrower output distribution produces more uniform behavior across input complexity levels.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the 50\% Hypothesis Failed}

The 50\% hypothesis was motivated by the intuition that a tight structural feedback loop would dramatically narrow the model's effective output space. This intuition was partially correct but fundamentally incomplete. Schema constraints do narrow the \emph{structural} output space: they eliminate Type 1 failures reliably and catch the malformed JSON that would otherwise break downstream systems. What they cannot narrow is the \emph{semantic} output space---the variance in how the model reads, interprets, and synthesizes input text into categorical and free-form outputs. At $T{=}1.0$, semantic variance dominates. Structural validators are blind to it by design.

The finding is consistent with Tam et al.\ \cite{tam2024let}, who argue that the fundamental bottleneck in structured IE is semantic alignment rather than syntactic compliance. Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2024we} similarly distinguish between ``low-level'' constraints (format, structure) and ``high-level'' constraints (semantics, style), noting that practitioners overwhelmingly need both but existing tooling predominantly addresses the former. The results in this paper give that distinction a concrete empirical magnitude: on a realistic IE task, the high-level gap is roughly $4\times$ larger than the low-level gap that schema validation can close.

\subsection{Implications for Production IE Pipelines}

The results translate into four concrete recommendations for teams building production information extraction systems.

\paragraph{Schema constraints are necessary but insufficient.}
They should remain in production pipelines as a sanity check that eliminates Type 1 structural failures. They should not be relied on as the primary mechanism for achieving output consistency.
\textbf{What to do next:} Keep the schema validator. Add a separate stability monitor that compares outputs across independent runs for a subset of inputs, so drift in semantic consistency is detectable even when structural validation continues to pass.

\paragraph{Model selection matters more than constraint architecture.}
The SLM-vs-LLM stability gap (26--29\%) is larger than the constrained-vs-unconstrained gap within either model (at most 11.9\%). For stability-critical pipelines, a smaller, more predictable model may be preferable to a larger, more expressive one---and is also substantially cheaper to operate.
\textbf{What to do next:} Before investing engineering effort in retry loops, benchmark candidate models on cross-run consistency for the target schema. The choice of model is likely to have higher leverage than the choice of enforcement strategy.

\paragraph{Canonicalization is a first-class engineering concern.}
Residual semantic volatility in free-form fields (e.g., \texttt{regional\_warnings}) is driven by surface-form variation rather than by structural failure. No schema validator can address this.
\textbf{What to do next:} Add a post-generation entity normalization layer. For geographic terms, a named entity linker backed by a canonical gazetteer (GeoNames, Wikidata) collapses surface variants into stable identifiers. For domain-specific entities, a fuzzy string matcher against a controlled vocabulary is a lighter-weight alternative.

\paragraph{Temperature settings require calibration, not defaults.}
The $T{=}1.0$ setting used here is a deliberate stress test. Production pipelines should use lower temperatures (e.g., $T{=}0.3$--$0.5$) for structured IE tasks. However, even at $T{=}0$ full determinism is not guaranteed for large models on distributed hardware \cite{atil2024nondeterminism, yuan2025numerical}.
\textbf{What to do next:} Treat temperature as a tunable parameter with measurable consequences on cross-run consistency. Sweep a small range and report stability at each setting rather than adopting a default.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Three limitations bound the scope of the conclusions drawn here, and each points to a concrete direction for follow-up work.

\paragraph{Single task domain.} The evaluation covers one real-world task (geopolitical travel risk) with one strict schema. Generalizing the findings to other IE domains---financial compliance extraction, clinical coding, legal contract parsing---would require replicating the constrained-vs-unconstrained comparison on those schemas. The capacity-consistency tradeoff in particular may behave differently when the target schema has a larger categorical vocabulary or more hierarchical structure. \emph{Future direction:} a cross-domain stability benchmark that applies the Row Jaccard methodology to at least three structurally distinct IE schemas.

\paragraph{Retroactive constraints only.} This paper evaluates schema validation with error-feedback retry. Proactive constrained decoding (grammar-constrained logit masking) may achieve higher stability at comparable cost by preventing structural violations at generation time rather than correcting them retroactively. The two approaches have not been compared head-to-head on cross-run consistency. \emph{Future direction:} a controlled comparison of retroactive retry vs.\ proactive constrained decoding on the same pipeline, measuring both stability gain and end-to-end latency.

\paragraph{Token cost, not wall-clock latency.} The cost analysis in Section~\ref{sec:cost} estimates token overhead from retry counts but does not measure wall-clock latency, which depends on API infrastructure (batching, caching, concurrency) outside the experimental control of this study. A production team evaluating whether to deploy retry loops needs both numbers, not just tokens. \emph{Future direction:} instrument the pipeline with wall-clock timing under realistic concurrency loads and report latency distributions alongside token costs.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper evaluated whether schema validation with error-feedback retry---the default engineering response to LLM output volatility---meaningfully reduces cross-run inconsistency in a realistic structured information extraction pipeline. The evaluation was conducted on a live Travel Risk Assessment Pipeline spanning 30 countries, two model scales, and two experimental conditions, with all inputs cached to isolate model stochasticity from input drift.

Three findings define the contribution:

\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{Schema constraints deliver modest stability, not dominant stability.} The 50\% volatility reduction hypothesis was robustly rejected. Constraints produced at most an 11.9\% improvement for the SLM and no statistically significant improvement for the LLM.

 \item \textbf{Smaller models are more stable than larger ones at high temperature.} The 8B SLM was 26--29\% more consistent across runs than the 120B LLM in every tested condition---a capacity-consistency tradeoff with direct consequences for model selection in production IE pipelines.

 \item \textbf{The canonicalization gap is the dominant residual.} Semantically equivalent surface forms in free-form fields (\texttt{"Arauca"} vs.\ \texttt{"Arauca Department, Colombia"}) account for most of the volatility that remains after schema enforcement. Structural validators are architecturally incapable of closing this gap.
\end{enumerate}

The practical implication is narrow and concrete. Schema validation belongs in production IE pipelines as a structural safety net, but teams that require genuine output consistency must invest in semantic grounding---entity linking, canonicalization layers, controlled vocabularies---as a first-class component of their reliability architecture, not as an optional refinement.
